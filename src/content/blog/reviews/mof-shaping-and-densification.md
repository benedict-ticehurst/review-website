## Abstract

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are usually reported as powders, but storage and separation devices are volume-constrained and need robust, formed solids. This review examines how shaping and densification change MOF performance, with a focus on volumetric metrics. It covers uniaxial pressing, binder-based pelletisation and granulation, additive manufacturing, spray drying, binder-free sol–gel monoliths and MOF glasses. The evidence shows that volumetric capacity depends on real packing density, not single-crystal density [2, 3]. Pressing can severely damage some frameworks, with reported HKUST-1 surface-area losses of about 27–76% depending on pressure [5]. Binder-free sol–gel monoliths report the largest documented volumetric gains, for example 259 versus 180 cm³(STP) cm⁻³ of methane for a HKUST-1 monolith relative to powder [13]. The review organises the value of shaping around six properties: volumetric working capacity, packing density, mechanical strength, kinetics and pressure drop, stability under realistic conditions, and cost. It argues that the largest gaps are the reliance of volumetric design rules on single-crystal density, the scarcity of realistic-condition tests on shaped bodies, and the near-absence of techno-economic models built on shaped properties. It also argues that the best-documented scaled-up MOF in this evidence base, CALF-20 [28], is a benchmark that monolith routes need to be compared against, and that the form in which it is scaled up should be confirmed before that comparison is drawn.

## 1. Introduction

Most MOF research reports adsorption properties for crystalline powders, but practical devices such as storage tanks, packed beds and contactors need formed solids. This has driven a body of work on what shaping does to MOF properties. Studies motivated explicitly by industrial use, such as Bazer-Bachi et al. [5] and the kilogram-scale granulation study of Hindocha and Poulston [6], examine how forming changes surface area, crystallinity and adsorption capacity. The recurring finding is that shaping is not neutral: it can preserve, degrade or, in some binder-free routes, improve volumetric performance depending on the MOF and the method [5, 6, 13].

This review draws on a compiled set of 29 sources covering the volumetric versus gravimetric trade-off [1–3], binder-based shaping [4–11], binder-free densification and monoliths [12–19], MOF glasses and crystal–glass composites [20, 21], thermal conductivity [22–24], and techno-economics, process-level evaluation, and computational screening and classification [25–29]. The set is a curated selection of key papers, not the output of a systematic search, so statements about what the literature "lacks" describe this evidence base and should be tested against a wider search before being presented as field-wide conclusions. One further caution applies throughout. Volumetric capacities are reported as total, excess or working (deliverable) capacity, and calculated on either bulk (shaped-body) or single-crystal density. These are not interchangeable, and the size of a "gain" is highly sensitive to which baseline and capacity type is compared. Numbers below are given as the sources report them, with conditions where known.

## 2. Why Volumetric Performance Governs Deployment

The case for caring about volumetric performance starts with the observation that maximising gravimetric properties does not maximise volumetric ones. In their analysis of methane storage, Peng et al. found that a void fraction of about 0.8 and gravimetric surface areas of roughly 2500–3000 m² g⁻¹ gave the highest methane capacities, and that methane uptake decreased beyond that optimal surface area [2]. The same work estimated that a MOF with a surface area of 7500 m² g⁻¹ and a pore volume of 3.2 cm³ g⁻¹ could reach the DOE gravimetric target of 0.5 g g⁻¹ while delivering around 200 cm³ cm⁻³ volumetric uptake [2]. Chen et al. addressed the same tension directly with NU-1501-Al, a material reported to surpass DOE gravimetric methane and hydrogen targets while retaining balanced volumetric uptake [1]. For hydrogen, Gómez-Gualdrón et al. reported that volumetric uptake is maximised at a single-crystal-density "sweet spot" of about 0.5–0.6 g cm⁻³ [3]. This review's framing, which is an interpretation rather than a claim made by these papers, is that for volume-constrained systems the size of the tank or contactor is set by how much adsorbate a given volume of *formed* material can hold. Volumetric performance is therefore the more deployment-relevant metric, and the studies above [1–3] are the natural starting point for evaluating shaping.

A recurring caveat, however, is that volumetric figures for new materials are usually calculated from ideal single-crystal densities. Peng et al. state this directly: the volumetric uptake values in their analysis are based on ideal single-crystal densities, whereas in reality the packing densities of MOFs are much lower [2]. Chen et al. and Gómez-Gualdrón et al. likewise treat crystal-density-based quantities [1, 3]. This gap between crystal density and achievable bulk density is the pivot of the review. Whether shaping narrows it (dense, well-packed bodies) or widens the performance loss (binder dilution, framework collapse) determines how much of a material's theoretical volumetric advantage survives in a device. The same paper that flags the density gap also reports that compacting HKUST-1 into wafer shapes partially collapses the framework, decreasing both volumetric and gravimetric uptake significantly [2].

## 3. Shaping as a Spectrum: From Pressing Damage to Binder-Free Monoliths

Shaping is best treated as a spectrum of routes with different trade-offs, not as a binary choice between binders and monoliths. This section moves from routes that carry the largest documented penalties to routes that report the largest gains, and ends with a critical reading of headline claims.

### 3.1 Pressing and compaction

The most direct evidence on shaping comes from studies of uniaxial pressing, and it shows large differences between frameworks. Peterson et al. studied the effect of pelletisation pressure on Cu₃(BTC)₂ (HKUST-1) and UiO-66 and reported that the BET surface area of HKUST-1 dropped by about 47% at 69 MPa [4]. Bazer-Bachi et al. examined ZIF-8, HKUST-1 and SIM-1 by compression and binder pelletisation [5]. HKUST-1 lost about 27% of its surface area at roughly 24 MPa (1897 to 1377 m² g⁻¹) and about 76% at roughly 400 MPa (down to 453 m² g⁻¹), whereas ZIF-8 was far more tolerant, keeping its crystallinity up to about 230 MPa with only about 11% BET loss (1433 to 1278 m² g⁻¹), and its catalytic activity per unit mass remained constant [5]. This strong dependence on the MOF means that pressing outcomes cannot be generalised across the family [5].

Sutton et al. help explain why pressing damages porosity by separating intrinsic from extrinsic mechanical stability in Zn-MOF-74. The framework stayed crystalline to above 2.8 GPa under hydrostatic pressure (bulk modulus about 15 GPa), yet its BET surface area fell at only 0.08–0.77 GPa under uniaxial pelletisation, with shear yield estimated at 0.16–0.50 GPa [18]. The authors' framing is that porosity loss during pelletisation is driven by shear rather than simple compression [18].

Mechanical densification can nevertheless help, up to a limit. Zacharia et al. densified MOF-177 mechanically and reported an excess volumetric hydrogen capacity of 25.8 g L⁻¹ (about 6 MPa, 77 K), roughly 78% higher than the powder, but also progressive amorphisation above about 0.51 g cm⁻³ and a fall in gravimetric capacity as density rises [19]. Together, these studies show that pressing gives real volumetric gains in some frameworks and severe porosity loss in others [5, 19]. This is why binder-free approaches that avoid high mechanical stress (Section 3.3) are of interest.

### 3.2 Binder-based and additively manufactured bodies

Binder-based routes are the incumbent approach and generally trade some capacity for robustness. At kilogram scale, Hindocha and Poulston formed MIL-100(Fe), Cu-BTC and CPO-27(Ni) by dry and wet granulation and tested ammonia adsorption for respiratory-protection filters [6]. Reported ammonia capacities were 47 mg g⁻¹ for MIL-100(Fe) and 62 mg g⁻¹ for CPO-27(Ni), while Cu-BTC fell from 97 to 37 mg g⁻¹ on forming and decomposed at 25 °C and 80% relative humidity [6]. Outcomes therefore depend strongly on the framework, and realistic ageing and humidity tests can expose failures that fresh-sample data hide [6].

Higher-loading formulations can limit the penalty. Zheng et al. shaped SIFSIX, TIFSIX and GeFSIX materials, HKUST-1, Mg-MOF-74 and MIL-101 using a poly(vinyl butyral) (PVB) binder at up to 95% MOF loading, and reported small capacity reductions: C₂H₂ uptake decreased by 0.56–0.93% and C₂H₄ uptake by 5–12.5%, while the IAST C₂H₂/C₂H₄ selectivity was reported as 116.4, about 35% higher than the powder [7]. The binder still occupies mass and volume, however, and for CO₂ capture a study of CALF-20 pellets compared polysulfone, PVC, PVF and cellulose acetate binders and reported a CO₂ capacity loss of about 20% for the polysulfone route, with cellulose acetate giving the best balance of mechanical strength and uptake [8]. Capacity penalties of order 20% are therefore realistic for binder-based CO₂ adsorbents [8].

Additive manufacturing offers a way to make self-supporting structured bodies. Thakkar et al. robocasted MOF-74(Ni) and UTSA-16(Co) monoliths at 80–85 wt% MOF loading [9]. The printed monoliths retained 79% and 87% of the powder CO₂ uptake (1.35 and 1.31 mmol g⁻¹ respectively, at 5000 ppm and 25 °C) and were presented as self-standing bodies with low pressure drop, at a cost of 13–21% capacity relative to powder [9]. Wang et al. printed MIL-101(Cr)/cellulose acetate monoliths loaded with polyethylenimine (PEI) for direct air capture and reported 1.05 mmol g⁻¹ CO₂ uptake at −20 °C and 400 ppm, with kinetics not compromised relative to powder [10]. This is one of the few results in the evidence base obtained at conditions relevant to direct air capture, although the inactive support acts as dead weight [10]. Spray drying, finally, was presented by Carné-Sánchez et al. as a continuous, scalable route that combines synthesis and shaping: nanoscale MOFs (including Cu-BTC) assembled into hollow spherical superstructures below 5 µm, although such hollow structures are inherently low in density [11].

### 3.3 Binder-free sol–gel monoliths and related routes

Binder-free routes aim to form the shaped body from the MOF itself, avoiding both the mass penalty of an inert binder and the damage of high-pressure compaction. Much of the binder-free monolith work in this evidence base comes from a single research group (Fairen-Jimenez and collaborators, University of Cambridge) [12–16]. The evidence for monoliths is therefore concentrated, and independent replication by other groups would strengthen the conclusions drawn from it (an interpretation, not a claim made by the sources).

The earliest result in this line is that of Tian et al., who made binder-free ZIF-8 monoliths whose bulk density and volumetric BET surface area were about three times those of the powder, with the micropore system reported as intact and the material more rigid than single crystals [12]. This was presented as the first high-density binder-free ZIF monolith, although the samples were small [12]. The same group later reported a sol–gel monolithic HKUST-1 (monoHKUST-1) of about 1 cm³, with a methane uptake of 259 cm³(STP) cm⁻³ at 65 bar and 298 K, compared with 180 cm³(STP) cm⁻³ for the powder [13]. The monolith had a bulk density of 1.06 g cm⁻³, a Young's modulus of 9.3 GPa and a hardness of 460 MPa, reported as about 130% higher hardness (the comparison baseline should be confirmed in the source) [13]. This is described as a record volumetric methane capacity for a conformed solid, though the bulk density remains below the crystal density [13]. From the reported values, the methane gain is about 44% (259/180, my calculation), which is well short of a doubling of capacity (see Section 3.4).

Gel-based processing provides a route beyond HKUST-1. Bueken et al. showed that zirconium MOFs (UiO-66-X, UiO-67, MOF-801/808 and NU-1000) can be processed through gel routes into xerogel and aerogel monoliths with hierarchical micro- and mesoporosity, and into 600 µm UiO-66 spheres by an oil-drop method, presented as a proof-of-principle precursor to later monolith work [17]. Connolly et al. then produced binder-free UiO-66 monoliths without applied pressure and tuned bulk density between 0.43 and 1.05 g cm⁻³ by controlling mesoporosity and drying [14]. Reported methane working capacities were 172 cm³ cm⁻³ (5–65 bar) and 261 cm³ cm⁻³ (5–100 bar), with total methane capacities of 211 cm³ cm⁻³ (65 bar) and 296 cm³ cm⁻³ (100 bar) and a CO₂ total capacity of 284 cm³ cm⁻³ (40 bar), all at 298 K [14]. The authors reported that tuned mesoporosity allowed the monolith to exceed values predicted from grand-canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) single-crystal maxima, although the route requires precise control of drying [14].

The same approach has been extended to hydrogen and water. Madden et al. reported densified monoHKUST-1 storing 46 g L⁻¹ hydrogen at 50 bar and 77 K and delivering 41–42 g L⁻¹ over a 25–50 bar swing, with operating pressures up to 80% lower than a benchmark material, although these results are cryogenic [15]. For water adsorption, Çamur et al. reported monolithic UiO-66, UiO-66-NH₂ and Zr-fumarate [16]. The Zr-fumarate monolith had a bulk density of 0.76 g cm⁻³ with no loss of adsorption performance, and a coefficient of performance (COP) of 0.71 at regeneration temperatures of 100 °C or below, which is relevant to heating and cooling applications, although durability under repeated water cycling at scale remains to be demonstrated [16].

MOF glasses offer a different route to dense, processable bodies. Ma and Horike's review classifies network-forming MOF glasses and describes retention of short-range order after melt-quenching, with the caveats that most MOFs do not melt and that porosity is lost [20]. Crystal–glass composites embed a crystalline MOF (MIL-53) in a ZIF-62 glass matrix, preserving the crystalline phase and improving mechanical properties, although accessible porosity is reduced [21]. These routes are conceptually relevant to shaping but sit further from the volumetric-capacity results discussed above [20, 21].

### 3.4 Reading headline gains critically

The gains reported for binder-free monoliths are real but are defined relative to specific baselines, and the baseline matters. The ZIF-8 result is about three times the powder volumetric BET surface area [12]. The HKUST-1 methane result is 259 versus 180 cm³(STP) cm⁻³ at 65 bar and 298 K, about 44% higher (calculated from [13]). The hydrogen result is stated relative to a benchmark, with up to 80% lower operating pressure [15], and the Zr-fumarate water result is no adsorption loss relative to the powder at a bulk density of 0.76 g cm⁻³ [16]. This review's interpretation, which is an inference from how the sources define their comparisons and not a claim made by them, is that monolith gains are largest when measured against poorly packed powders and vary strongly with the material, adsorbate and metric. They should not be read as universal multipliers. Comparisons against the best binder-based route, or against a crystal-density maximum, are less common in this evidence base, and Connolly et al. is a notable exception in comparing to GCMC single-crystal predictions [14].

## 4. Six Key Properties That Characterise the Value of Shaping

Drawing the evidence together, the value of a shaping or densification route is best characterised by six properties, with thermal conductivity acting as a supporting property where heat of adsorption must be managed. The table summarises representative evidence for each, and the paragraphs that follow discuss how strongly the evidence base speaks to them.

| # | Property | Representative evidence in this review | Refs |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Volumetric (working) capacity at real bulk density | monoHKUST-1 259 vs 180 cm³ cm⁻³ CH₄; UiO-66 monolith working capacity 172 cm³ cm⁻³ (5–65 bar); monoHKUST-1 46 g L⁻¹ H₂ | [13–15] |
| 2 | Packing / bulk density relative to crystal density | Bulk densities of 1.06 g cm⁻³ (HKUST-1), 0.43–1.05 g cm⁻³ (tunable UiO-66), 0.76 g cm⁻³ (Zr-fumarate) | [13, 14, 16] |
| 3 | Mechanical strength and attrition resistance | HKUST-1 monolith E = 9.3 GPa, H = 460 MPa; Zn-MOF-74 shear yield 0.16–0.50 GPa | [13, 18] |
| 4 | Kinetics and pressure drop | Self-standing printed monoliths with low pressure drop; kinetics not compromised in printed amine-loaded monoliths | [9, 10] |
| 5 | Stability under realistic conditions | Cu-BTC decomposition at 25 °C / 80% RH; CALF-20 durability to steam and acid gases | [6, 28] |
| 6 | Cost / techno-economics | Structured-MOF CO₂ capture costs of about $60.6–80.2 per tonne CO₂ | [26] |

### 4.1 Capacity, density and strength

Volumetric capacity at the bulk density of the formed body is the central measure of shaping success, and it is tied closely to how much of a framework's crystal-density potential the shaped body realises. Binder-free monoliths report the highest values in this evidence base (Section 3.3) [13–15], reaching bulk densities of 1.06 g cm⁻³ for HKUST-1 [13], 0.43–1.05 g cm⁻³ for tunable UiO-66 [14] and 0.76 g cm⁻³ for Zr-fumarate [16], whereas binder-based bodies typically report capacity retention relative to powder in the range of about 79–99% depending on the system [7, 9]. Density alone is not enough. Mechanical densification of MOF-177 raised volumetric hydrogen capacity but led to amorphisation above about 0.51 g cm⁻³ [19], so higher density is only useful while the pore system survives [5, 18, 19]. Because the definition (total, excess or working) and the baseline change the apparent size of any gain, comparisons should be made like for like [13, 14].

Mechanical robustness is what makes a formed body usable in a bed or tank. The HKUST-1 monolith is reported with a Young's modulus of 9.3 GPa and a hardness of 460 MPa [13], and ZIF-8 monoliths were described as more rigid than single crystals [12]. Strength is also tied to how bodies are made: Zn-MOF-74 pelletisation shows that shear during uniaxial pressing, not intrinsic framework weakness, can collapse porosity [18]. Robust monoliths made without high-pressure compaction may therefore avoid this mechanism, although this is an inference from combining [12, 13, 18].

### 4.2 Transport, stability, heat and cost

Shaped bodies must also let adsorbate reach the adsorbent without excessive pressure drop. Robocasted monoliths are presented as self-standing with low pressure drop [9], and amine-loaded printed MIL-101(Cr) monoliths were reported not to suffer compromised kinetics relative to powder [10]. Tuned mesoporosity in UiO-66 monoliths [14] and hierarchical micro/mesoporosity in Zr-MOF gels [17] are relevant design levers for transport. Quantitative packed-bed pressure-drop data comparing binder-free monoliths with powders and pellets are not a feature of the evidence base assembled here, and this is flagged as a gap in Section 5.

Stability has to be judged on the shaped body under conditions that matter. Granulated Cu-BTC decomposed at 25 °C and 80% relative humidity [6], showing that some MOFs are not deployable regardless of shaping route. The Zr-fumarate monolith showed no adsorption loss at a bulk density of 0.76 g cm⁻³, but water-cycling durability at scale remains to be shown [16]. The strongest stability benchmark in the evidence base is CALF-20, reported as durable to steam over more than 450,000 cycles and to wet NOx/SOx acid gases over 2000 h [28].

Adsorption is exothermic, so heat dissipation affects the performance of dense beds. Adding 10 wt% expanded natural graphite to MOF-5 pellets raised thermal conductivity from 0.10 to 0.56 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹ (about fivefold, at 298 K) with crystallinity and surface area preserved up to 10 wt% [22]. A HKUST-1 composite with graphite flakes, formed as a conformed monolith of 377 kg m⁻³, was reported to be protected against amorphisation and to give about 19 g L⁻¹ volumetric hydrogen (cryogenic, atmospheric pressure) [23]. A computational screen of 10,194 hypothetical MOFs found that high thermal conductivity was favoured by densities above 1.0 g cm⁻³, pores below 10 Å and four-connected nodes [24]. Thermal additives dilute active capacity, which is the trade-off to weigh [22].

Cost is where shaping ultimately has to pay off. An early techno-economic analysis for hydrogen and natural gas storage examined four MOFs (Ni- and Mg-MOF-74, MOF-5 and HKUST-1) and set a target of below $10 per kg of MOF at 2.5 million kg per year, but on a powder-cost basis [25]. A system-level study of structured MOF adsorbents for post-combustion CO₂ capture by vacuum/pressure swing adsorption reported costs of $72.5 per tonne CO₂ for structured UiO-66 (95% purity, 88.8% recovery), $60.6 per tonne at 80% recovery, $75 per tonne for MIL-101@GO and $80.2 per tonne for ZIF-8 [26]. A techno-economic study of UTSA-16(Zn) for wet flue gas CO₂ capture used thermal swing adsorption scale-up and costing and identified the absence of TSA adsorber costing [27].

## 5. Critical Gaps and Tensions

The following gaps are drawn from the evidence base assembled here and are framed as arguments for a critical review, not as exhaustive statements about the whole field.

The first is that volumetric design rules rest on single-crystal density. Design rules for volumetric capacity are usually derived from ideal single-crystal densities [1–3], and Peng et al. explicitly acknowledge that real packing densities are much lower [2]. Computational studies of MOF structure collections, such as the thermal-conductivity screen of hypothetical MOFs [24] and the classification of structures in the Cambridge Structural Database [29], are built on crystal structures. Whether they account for the density of a shaped body is not established by this evidence base and should be checked in the papers. A shaping-aware screen would need to model achievable bulk density, binder content and retained porosity, which the results in Section 3 show vary strongly with material and route [5, 13, 14].

The second is that testing of shaped bodies under realistic conditions is thin. Where shaped bodies have been tested under demanding conditions, the results are informative: ageing and humidity exposed Cu-BTC failure [6], and amine-loaded printed monoliths were tested at −20 °C and 400 ppm [10]. However, the binder-free monolith results in this evidence base are largely single-component gas or water uptake data, some at cryogenic temperature [12–16]. Within this evidence base, none reports humid-flue-gas cycling of a binder-free monolith at cycle counts approaching those reported for CALF-20 [28]. This is a statement about the assembled sources and should be checked against the wider literature. Water-cycling durability of the Zr-fumarate monolith at scale is also listed as remaining to be proven [16].

The third is that process-level and techno-economic models rarely use shaped properties. Early techno-economic work used powder-cost bases [25], while a structured-adsorbent system-level study [26] and a wet-flue-gas analysis [27] are rare examples of process-level evaluation closer to deployable forms. The reported structured-MOF costs of $60.6–80.2 per tonne CO₂ [26] show that such comparisons are possible, but the evidence base contains no like-for-like techno-economic comparison of powder, binder-based and binder-free monolith forms of the same MOF.

These gaps bear directly on how the best-documented scaled-up MOF should be read. The most prominent scaled-up MOF in this evidence base is CALF-20, reported as durable over more than 450,000 steam cycles and over 2000 h in wet NOx/SOx acid gases, and as meeting a 95% CO₂ purity target [28]. The form in which CALF-20 is used at scale is not established by the sources summarised here. A separate study examines binder-based CALF-20 pellets [8], and whether the scaled-up form is a coated or structured contactor should be confirmed from [28] or other sources before it is used in an argument. This review's analytical point, which is an inference and not a claim by any single source, is conditional: if the best-documented scaled-up route proves to be a structured or binder-based form rather than a monolith, any argument for monoliths must engage with why. Possible explanations include heat and mass transfer, manufacturability and cost, and these cannot be resolved from the present evidence.

Three developments would change the argument, and these are this review's analytical benchmarks, offered as tests of the monolith thesis and not as findings from the sources. The first would be a binder-free monolith shown to match the humid steam and acid-gas durability reported for CALF-20 [28] at comparable cycle counts. The second would be a techno-economic analysis using *shaped* properties that places a monolith-based system in or below the $60.6–80.2 per tonne CO₂ range reported for structured MOF adsorbents [26]. The third would be evidence of prolonged cycling of monoliths without attrition, complementing the mechanical data in [13] and the shear-driven collapse mechanism in [18].

## 6. Conclusion

The literature supports a clear central message: volumetric performance at real bulk density, not crystal-density projections or gravimetric metrics, governs the value of a MOF in a volume-constrained system [1–3]. Shaping matters because it moves a MOF towards or away from its crystal-density potential. Pressing can be highly damaging for some frameworks [4, 5, 18], binder-based routes generally trade capacity for robustness [6–10], and binder-free sol–gel monoliths report the largest volumetric gains for several MOFs and adsorbates [12–16]. Those gains are baseline-dependent and MOF-specific, and the reported HKUST-1 methane gain is about 44% rather than a doubling [13].

The six properties in Section 4 (volumetric working capacity, packing density, mechanical strength, kinetics and pressure drop, stability under realistic conditions, and cost) provide a framework for comparing routes. The evidence base is strongest on capacity and density, thinner on kinetics and pressure drop, and thinnest on realistic-condition durability and shaped-property techno-economics [6, 10, 16, 25–28]. Closing those gaps, and benchmarking monolith routes against the best-documented scaled-up MOF [28], are the most defensible directions for a critical treatment of shaping.
