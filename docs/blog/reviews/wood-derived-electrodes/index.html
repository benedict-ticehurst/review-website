Wood-Derived Electrodes: A Solution for Low Tortuosity Electrodes

Benedict D Ticehurst Yr 3 Master of Chemistry, Chemistry and Chemical Engineering, University of Southampton

Abstract

The ever-increasing demand for grid-scale and portable energy storage has intensified the search for electrode architectures that combine high power density with sustainable manufacturing. This review critically examines conventional supercapacitor and battery electrodes, highlighting how high tortuosity in their porous networks limits ion transport; and surveys wood-templated strategies that exploit the intrinsic, aligned channels of natural wood to produce low-tortuosity, self-supporting carbon electrodes. We compare two main wood-templating approaches (carbonised wood for supercapacitors; sol–gel infiltration for battery cathodes), demonstrating that wood-derived electrodes can achieve specific capacitances and rate capabilities on par with traditional designs while eliminating binders and heavy current collectors. We then appraise emerging low-tortuosity techniques, magnetic templating and 3D printing of bespoke carbon “inks”, that offer precise pore control but face challenges in scalability and ink formulation. Finally, we identify key research gaps, including structural reproducibility, large-area uniformity, and the need for cradle-to-grave life-cycle assessments, and propose future directions to standardise protocols and integrate greener activation methods. Collectively, wood-templating and allied additive-manufacturing approaches represent promising pathways toward next-generation, environmentally friendly energy-storage electrodes.

1. Introduction

Given the urgent need to expand grid-scale storage beyond its current capacity, simply ramping up lithium-ion battery production would require building extensive new manufacturing plants, securing vastly larger raw-material supply chains, and overhauling global logistics; an infrastructure undertaking of unprecedented scale [1]. Energy demands will only continue to grow, and this challenge motivates the search for fundamentally different storage technologies that are sustainable while minimising mineral exploitation and waste production [2]. Concurrently, as governments and industries commit to carbon neutrality, deploying renewable, regenerative feedstocks in energy devices can substantially lower lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions compared to mineral intensive chemistries.

This review examines current charge storage technologies which struggle due to their high-tortuosity structure. Tortuosity (τ) in electrodes is a dimensionless parameter that quantifies the complexity and elongation of ion transport pathways through a porous medium relative to its thickness; defined as the ratio of the actual ions’ pathway length to the straight-line distance across the electrode. Higher tortuosity indicates more convoluted and indirect pathways, which increase ionic resistance, limiting effective diffusivity, and constrain the rate performance of electrochemical cells. Minimising tortuosity is crucial for achieving high power densities, as it facilitates faster ion transport and reduces concentration polarisation under high current loads.

However, no prior review has systematically compared wood-templating with additive-manufacturing approaches for tortuosity control. Additionally, this review delves into the intricacies of using wood as a template for fabricating these critically low-tortuosity electrodes; later comparing this method with other emerging techniques such as magnetic templating and 3D printing which both aim to produce low-tortuosity electrodes. Finally, the potential applications of wood-derived electrodes are considered, along with their integration into commercial systems. As an emerging field of study, there are numerous unexplored areas in wood templating that must be addressed to enhance the practicality of this research.

2. Current Charge Storage Methods

2.1. Battery Electrode

Batteries consist of two electrodes (anode and cathode) immersed in an electrolyte. When they contact the electrolyte, each electrode develops a potential, and the resulting voltage difference drives current through an external circuit. Batteries store energy chemically, oxidation–reduction reactions at the electrode–electrolyte interface convert between electrical and chemical energy, with the extent of reaction governed by Faraday’s laws of electrolysis.

In conventional battery energy-storage systems (BESS), thin electrodes dominate because thick electrodes suffer from slower ion transport, leading to reduced charge/discharge [3]. Thick electrodes with a high areal capacity are preferred in battery designs because they can effectively increase the percentage of active mass, thereby enhancing the energy density in the device, further discussed in section 4. Subsequently, decreasing the manufacturing costs due to fewer steps required to achieve the total energy storage capacity. However, in solid-state batteries, ions must navigate grain boundaries, defects, and irregular channels which limits power output that is necessary for responsive energy storage [4].

Conventional lithium-ion battery electrodes are manufactured through a sequential wet-processing route to ensure high uniformity and scalability. First, active particles, such as lithium cobalt oxide for cathodes or graphite for anodes, are combined with conductive additives (e.g., carbon black) and a polymeric binder (PVDF/NMP) to form a stable slurry. The slurry’s rheological properties are tuned to prevent sedimentation and ensure consistent coating, often assessed via shear-rate and oscillatory rheometry [5]. Next, the homogenised slurry is deposited onto metal foil current collectors, aluminium for cathodes and copper for anodes, using precision techniques such as doctor-blade or slot-die coating to achieve target wet thicknesses of 100–200 µm [6]. The coated electrodes undergo a controlled drying step, typically at 60–120 °C under vacuum or inert gas, to remove NMP and solidify the binder network without causing binder migration or electrode cracking. Following drying, electrodes are calendared between heated rollers to reduce porosity to 30–40% and increase electrode density, thereby improving particle contact and electronic conductivity [7]. Finally, a secondary bake at ~120 °C is applied to eliminate residual moisture before the electrodes are cut to width and stacked with separators in inert-atmosphere gloveboxes for cell assembly [8]. This well-established process balances energy-density, power capability, and cycle life, but relies heavily on energy-intensive solvent handling and precise process control to maintain electrode quality at large production scales.

Although these conventional methods are optimised for high-volume production, they do introduce some notable drawbacks compared to wood-templated practices. For instance, the metal foils used as current collectors can account for a significant portion of the battery’s overall weight (up to 18% in some cases) [9], reducing the effective energy density since a portion of the electrode mass is inactive. In addition, the use of binders and other non-electroactive additives dilutes the proportion of active material, further limiting the energy-to-weight ratio. Moreover, slurry coating techniques can encounter challenges such as non-uniform deposition, inadequate adhesion, and the potential for delamination during long-term cycling, which may negatively affect the mechanical stability and overall cell performance [10]; all of these flaws stem from a deliberate reduction in binder content to reduce costs and decrease the weight of the electrode.

These challenges have spurred interest in alternative fabrication methods, most notably wood templating, that can both reduce inactive mass and simplify electrode manufacturing [11]. By leveraging the natural, hierarchical pore structure of wood (aligned xylem vessels), wood-templated electrodes inherently form directional microchannels that dramatically reduce tortuosity [12]; eliminating the need for heavy current collectors, binders, and energy- or solvent-intensive slurry casting [13]. These pre-formed channels enable rapid ion transport through ultrathick electrodes [14], unlocking higher areal capacities without sacrificing rate performance, while also improving atom economy and minimising reagent use (further discussed in section 4.2).

2.2. Supercapacitors

Supercapacitors (also called electric double-layer capacitors, electrochemical capacitors, or ultracapacitors) likewise use electrodes and an electrolyte, but instead of redox reactions they store charge electrostatically. Ions from the electrolyte form oppositely charged layers (Helmholtz layers) at the electrode surfaces, creating a molecular “dielectric.” Because this double-layer mechanism avoids slow chemical reactions, supercapacitors deliver very high power (rapid charge/discharge) but have lower energy density than batteries.

The grid requires supercapacitors (SCs) to maintain a stable frequency of 50 Hz [15]. If there is a sudden mismatch in supply and demand, an ever-increasing issue with the rising usage of intermittent renewable energy sources, SCs release or absorb small amounts of energy very quickly to maintain a consistent supply–demand equilibrium.

In the initial stage of electrode fabrication, pre-made high surface area porous carbon frameworks, generally graphene derivatives, are combined with a blend containing small amounts of conductive additives and a polymeric binder (e.g. polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) dispersed in a solvent, N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP)) to form a homogeneous, rheologically tuned slurry [16]. This slurry is then deposited onto thin metal foils, aluminium for positive electrodes; stainless steel, nickel, copper, or even flexible carbon-fibre paper for negative electrodes, using precision coating such as doctor blade, slot-die, or ultrasonic spray coating to achieve uniform film thicknesses typically ~10 µm [17]. After solvent removal through controlled drying under vacuum or inert gas at 60–120 °C, the electrode layers undergo calendaring, optimising electronic pathways by tailoring the micro- and macro-architecture for rapid ion transport. Electrodes are trimmed and assembled with formats along a porous separator and an electrolyte that is vacuum infiltrated to complete wetting of the carbon network [18]. Finally, quality-control protocols including mass-loading mapping, or sheet-resistance are applied before implemented sealing, yielding a SC with high density, longevity, and safety characteristics that underpin their diverse applications in grid stabilisation and power buffering [19].

Using a three-electrode setup (saturated calomel reference, Pt counter) in a 1 M Na₂SO₄/10 wt% PVA gel electrolyte, electrodes were prepared by doctor blade coating slurries of activated carbon, 5 wt% composite binder (CBE), and carbon black onto Al foil and dried at 60–70 °C. Cyclic voltammetry (–0.2–0.8 V) at scan rates from 5 to 100 mV/s yielded peak specific capacitances of 203 F/g for 5% CBE; at 100 mV/s these fell to 100 F/g with an energy density of 6.4 Wh/kg at a power density around 1.0 kW/kg, metrics typical of PVA-gel–based activated-carbon SCs. Galvanostatic charge/discharge cycling at 1 A/g (±0.8 V window) showed capacity retentions of 90.1% for 5% CBE over 1,000 cycles.

Current methods for constructing the pre-made porous carbon frameworks required in current SC manufacturing rely on sacrificial templates to generate the porous carbon framework. An approach that results in excess waste and energy consumption; advancing processes with improved atom economy and minimal reagent use is critical for scalable, environmentally responsible production [20].

3. Wood as a Template

3.1. Natural Abundance

As outlined in Figure 3, there are significant physio-chemical differences among wood species, primarily the lumen-to-cell wall ratio (the balance between the hollow internal cavity, lumen, and the surrounding structural material, cell wall) [22]. Following carbonisation, large lumens can become either macropores or mesopores, which can significantly increase the electrode’s surface area and pore volume. Lower lumen-to-cell wall ratios result in reduced surface area in exchange for higher density, providing greater resistance to compressive and tensile forces. Densities can range from 150–820 kg m⁻³; Young’s modulus varies from 3.71–18.76 GPa for Ochroma pyramidale and Eucalyptus, respectively [23]. Softwoods, such as Ochroma pyramidale (balsa wood), exhibit rapid growth, reaching 20 m in 7 years under favourable conditions [24]. Balsa is particularly valued for its thin-walled tracheid cells (90–95%) and large ray cell cavities (5–10%), which contribute to its unique properties. In contrast, hardwoods are typically slow growing; however, eucalyptus is an exception, as certain species can grow up to 30 metres in 6 years [25]. Despite this rapid growth, eucalyptus wood presents challenges due to its high extractive content, which promotes rapid shrinkage during drying, leading to structural deformation and collapse of essential pore networks. This collapse increases processing costs and ultimately limits the commercial viability of eucalyptus [26].

Diffuse-porous hardwoods like white alder, common birch, and sycamore maple exhibit high macroporosity (79%, 86%, 90%) with bimodal pore-size peaks near 0.7–7 µm, yielding large cumulative pore volumes suitable for rapid transport [27]. Ramin also shows a dominant macropore fraction (71%, peak ~400 nm), whereas Gaboon and Opepe achieve similar total porosity almost entirely through micropores, which can hinder infiltration [28]. At the low-macro-meso extreme, Macassar ebony and European boxwood are chiefly microporous with high density, while ring-porous Robinia balances fewer macropores with substantial mesoporosity; choosing among these species allows tuning the trade-off between pore accessibility, volume, and mechanical strength. Investigation into wood species remains limited: different hardwoods exhibit widely varying pore-size distributions, from Robinia’s 0.3 µm mesopores to ebony’s dominant <0.1 µm micropores. These differences critically impact electrolyte uptake and mechanical resilience, underscoring the need to match wood species to target performance metrics.

Summary of wood characteristics for the most representative wood species used in the energy storage field, comparing average dried weight, pricing, Young’s modulus, grain texture, wood-cell fibre orientation, and sustainability status (CITES/IUCN Red List) across species including pine, red cedar, spruce, basswood, balsa, poplar, beech, eucalyptus, birch, and Fraxinus mandshurica.

Continuous Cover Forestry (CCF) replaces wholesale clear-felling with the careful, selective removal of individual trees, preserving the forest canopy and root networks to promote natural regeneration, enhance biodiversity, and maintain soil integrity [29]. Unlike clear-cutting, which causes erosion, habitat loss, and carbon release, CCF sustains long-term carbon sequestration and delivers a steady stream of high-quality timber ideally suited for electrode templating [30].

Because CCF retains a mix of species and age classes, it also preserves a spectrum of wood architectures — from large-lumen softwoods to denser hardwoods — that researchers can exploit to fine-tune critical electrode parameters such as pore size distribution, electrical conductivity, and mechanical toughness [31]. These naturally hierarchical structures directly influence ion transport pathways and structural stability in carbonised templates. Integrating CCF into wood-templated electrode supply chains thus ensures that future SCs and batteries harness both peak electrochemical performance and genuine ecological sustainability. Future advancements in bio-based electrode materials will benefit from sustainable forestry practices, ensuring that wood-derived carbon remains a viable and eco-friendly alternative for energy storage applications, such as SCs and batteries. This synergy between forestry and materials science underscores the importance of integrated sustainability approaches, balancing resource extraction with long-term environmental and technological benefits.

3.2. Composition and Properties

Overcoming tortuous ion pathways in thick electrodes is essential for achieving both high-rate capability and high areal capacity in energy storage devices. Wood is a natural organic composite material characterised by a porous structure that spans several levels of hierarchy, ranging from millimetre to nanometre scales [32]; as one of the most abundant biomasses on Earth [33], it offers a renewable and sustainable platform for various material applications. Given that carbon-based materials are predominant in current battery technologies due to their high electrical conductivity, high reversibility, and chemical stability, wood-derived carbons offer a promising route toward greener energy storage solutions. Carbonised wood retains its unique mesoporous structure with straight channels oriented perpendicular to the cut cross-section, resulting in a strong template for electrodes suitable for batteries or a carbon framework for SCs [34]. This alignment minimises tortuosity within the electrode, allowing ions to travel through more direct and shorter pathways. The resulting electrolyte exhibits enhanced diffusion, leading to faster ion transport, which translates into improved rate capabilities and higher areal capacities, even when the electrode is made ultra-thick.

Although Shen et al. demonstrate limited capacity loss over 1,000 cycles for 1 mm thick wood-derived anodes, attributing this stability to the native xylem channels that keep tortuosity below 1.2 [21], Lu et al. report a 20% capacity fade after 5,000 cycles in electrodes of comparable thickness, implying that slow pore collapse under repeated cycling can undermine long-term performance [35]. To guard against this, enzyme-assisted hydrolysis selectively removes lignin and hemicellulose, widening mesopores by up to 35% (from 12–16 nm average pore diameter) and reinforcing the remaining cellulose scaffold [36]. The result is a carbon structure that combines lower tortuosity with higher mechanical integrity, delivering >90% capacity retention at 1 mm versus <80% for conventional particle-cast electrodes.

3.3. Wood as a self-standing electrode

Another key advantage of wood is its ability to serve as either a structural support or an electrical conductor, allowing it to be the base material for both SCs and batteries. These properties make wood an ideal material for the fabrication of free-standing electrodes. A free-standing electrode is one that maintains its structural integrity without requiring a supporting substrate or polymeric binder, in contrast to conventional electrodes, which are typically deposited onto metal current collectors [37]. To understand the potential of wood-derived carbon materials in energy storage, it is essential to examine the fundamental composition of raw wood. Raw wood is the fibrous tissue from trees containing three primary components: semicrystalline cellulose as the elementary fibrils; hemicellulose, which crosslinks the cellulose fibrils; and lignin, providing rigidity to the wood [38].

In conventional lithium-ion batteries, aluminium and copper foils serve as current collectors, contributing approximately 5% and 13%, respectively, to the total cell mass and collectively accounting for 18% of the weight of the battery [39]. These foils also incur material costs, approximately £2.70 kg⁻¹ for aluminium and £8.13 kg⁻¹ for copper. Eliminating these inactive components from the architecture can substantially increase the gravimetric energy density by allowing the entire electrode mass to serve as a charge carrier [35].

Recent studies have demonstrated alternative strategies for producing free-standing electrodes without wood-templating. For instance, Chen et al. (2022) developed a binder-rich, free-standing LiFePO₄ (LFP) cathode by first coating a conventional LFP slurry (80% active material, 10% Super P, 10% PVDF) onto carbon-coated aluminium foil, then chemically etching the foil with tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAH) at room temperature for 1 minute [20]. This etching selectively dissolved the aluminium, leaving behind a paper-like, flexible LFP film [40]. The resulting electrodes retained high electrochemical performance, delivering 151–154 mAh g⁻¹ over 50 cycles at 0.1–0.5 C with Coulombic efficiencies >99%, while enabling flexibility, mechanical resilience under bending, and reduced weight due to foil removal. Notably, impedance measurements revealed comparable or even improved charge-transfer resistance (Rct) relative to foil-supported controls.

While both approaches eliminate metal foils and enhance energy density, wood-templated electrodes offer additional structural advantages: they are inherently porous, aligned, and low-tortuosity, which supports fast ion transport across much thicker electrodes. In contrast, slurry-cast films remain compositionally heterogeneous and diffusion-limited as thickness increases. However, wood-templated systems still face challenges in achieving high areal mass loading and process scalability, areas where foil-etching methods maintain compatibility with existing slurry-coating infrastructure.

4. Synthesis of Electrode via Wood

4.1. Carbonised Wood Supercapacitors

Current SCs require sacrificial templating to construct a porous carbon framework; these approaches require solvent-intensive steps and generate substantial chemical waste [41]. By contrast, wood-templated SCs exploit the intrinsic cellular structure, thereby reducing environmental impact.

The synthesis process begins with the thermal pre-treatment of wood at 300 °C, which partially decomposes lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose, opening the tracheid channels. The pre-carbonised wood is then infused with a glucose-salt solution, allowing the salt to crystallise within the tracheids, forming cube-like structures. Controlled drying at various temperatures influences the crystal morphology of the salts, which in turn affects the final carbon structure [42]. Carbonisation at 1,000 °C in a tubular furnace under inert conditions (typically nitrogen), at a rate of 5 °C min⁻¹, converts glucose into carbon [43]. The subsequent removal of salt yields a structured wood-derived carbon electrode with an optimised pore architecture. The use of freeze-thaw pretreatment in combination with this process increases the specific surface area by 1.8 times and the pore volume by 3.58 times [44]. No acid-base activation was used, making this a greener and simpler method.

After pre-carbonisation, the tracheid cavities of the wood were filled with a solution containing 3.5 M glucose in a saturated salt solution at room temperature. This treatment, which modified the surface properties, promotes hydrogen bonding between the glucose and the pre-carbonised wood [45]. The resulting material exhibited remarkable stability, showing no performance degradation after 10,000 cycles, and achieved a capacitance of 2.2 F cm⁻² and an energy density of 0.69 mWh/kg [46].

Using a 1 M Na₂SO₄, 10 wt% PVA gel electrolyte, electrochemical tests were conducted in a three-electrode system with saturated Calomel Electrode and Platinum counter electrode, with symmetric electrode pairings [47]. Peak readings for RCWC-150 (Reconstructed Cubic Stacked Wood-derived Carbon, liquid electrolyte evaporated at 150 °C) were obtained using cyclic voltammetry scan rates ranging from 5 to 30 mV/s, achieving an areal capacitance of 6.75 F/cm² at 5 mA/cm², decreasing to 4.18 F/cm² at 30 mA/cm²; a volumetric capacitance of 53.0 F/cm³ at 5 mA/cm², dropping to 29.4 F/cm³ at 30 mA/cm²; and an energy density for the all-solid-state symmetric SC of 0.37 mWh/cm² and 7.36 mWh/kg at a power density of 2.5 mW/kg.

Wood-derived SCs match many performance metrics of conventional designs while dramatically simplifying fabrication and minimising chemical waste. This biomass-derived pathway offers a viable, sustainable alternative for next-generation energy-storage devices, especially where environmental footprint and production efficiency are critical. In contrast, current wood-based SCs often use wood pulp to form paper mats [48] (sometimes blended with graphene), which are vacuum-filtered, carbonised, and chemically activated to produce high-surface-area carbon papers.

4.2. Wood-Templated Battery Electrodes

While supercapacitors play a crucial role in maintaining grid stability due to their rapid charge-discharge capabilities, batteries remain the predominant energy storage devices, offering higher energy densities suitable for long-term applications [49]. In battery electrodes wood is used as a template instead of incorporating it into its structure. This is because low tortuosity directly enhances ionic transport, consistent with ion transport models where aligned, low-tortuosity structures significantly reduce transport distance and concentration polarisation under high-rate conditions. By shortening the true ionic pathway relative to the electrode thickness, this boosts effective diffusivity and reduces concentration polarisation under high-rate operation. Sol-injection is a fabrication technique where a liquid solution is infused into the porous pre-carbonated wood template [50]. After infiltration, the infused wood is calcined, leaving behind a solid electrode that preserves the original aligned channel structure of the wood, resulting in fibre-aligned thick (FAT) electrodes.

After the infusion of a LiCoO₂ (LCO) precursor sol, the infiltrated pinewood slice was heated in a vacuum oven at 90 °C for 72 h to evaporate the solvent, transforming the sol into a gel. The infiltration of LCO gel into the microchannels results in them becoming totally blocked by LCO precursor gel. The wood slice was sintered at 700 °C for 2 h in air to form bulk LCO cathode (LCO-1) [51]. LCO-1 is the initial bulk cathode obtained after the first cycle of this process. LCO-2 is an enhanced version of the cathode produced by repeating the sol–gel infusion and calcination processes; increased active material loading correlates with enhanced areal capacity in LCO-2. Thicker cathodes with optimised porosity can improve energy density without compromising ionic mobility when channel alignment is preserved. This process allows for better contact between the LCO structure and the electrolyte, facilitating more efficient ion transport and charge storage. However, despite these advantages, the high-temperature sintering step and multiple infusion cycles increase energy consumption and material costs, potentially limiting large-scale adoption [52].

Using a 1.0 M LiPF₆ electrolyte in a mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC), dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) at a weight ratio of 1:1:1, tests were conducted in lithium cathode half-cells utilising a Biologic VMP-3 potentiostat, with lithium serving as both counter and reference electrode. Galvanostatic charge–discharge measurements were performed by first charging the cells to 4.25 V at 0.05 C, then discharging at rates ranging from 0.05 to 1 C. The control LCO electrode exhibited poor performance, with lithium cobalt oxide (LCO) achieving only 65% capacity utilisation at 0.05 C and near-zero capacity at 1 C, attributed to high Li-ion transport resistance in the 1 mm-thick electrode. In contrast, the wood-templated LCO-1 and LCO-2 electrodes, featuring vertically aligned microchannels, showed significantly improved rate capabilities. LCO-1 demonstrated 70% of its initial capacity, delivering 73.7 mAh g⁻¹ at 1 C and an areal capacity of 13.3 mAh cm⁻² at 0.05 C. LCO-2 achieved 37.8 mAh g⁻¹ at 1 C and a higher areal capacity of 24.5 mAh cm⁻² due to its greater mass loading. Under the United States Advanced Battery Consortium (USABC) dynamic stress test, simulating electric vehicle operation with peak discharge rates up to 1.74 C, the LCO-2 electrode delivered 110.3 mAh g⁻¹ and a capacity of 22.7 mAh cm⁻². These FAT electrodes can outperform the commercial rate benchmark by nearly fourfold while maintaining lower polarisation throughout [53].

5. Alternative Methods for Low-Tortuosity Structures

5.1. Magnetic Templating

Conventional electrodes suffer from tortuous ion and electron pathways, limiting fast-charging performance and energy efficiency. Magnetic templating addresses this by creating vertically aligned, low-tortuosity pore structures that enhance lithium-ion and electron transport. This is achieved by using magnetic alignment of sacrificial pore-forming solid and liquid phases to directly produce anisotropic structures oriented normal to the electrode plane. The resulting vertical alignment improves transport properties, enabling fast-charging capabilities with specific capacities of 110 mAh g⁻¹ at 3 C and 76 mAh g⁻¹ at 4 C [54]. This approach highlights an alternative strategy for improving interfacial contact and stacking density, challenges that wood-derived templating also aims to solve.

Another approach involves the in-situ deposition of Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles onto dispersed Graphene Oxide (GO) sheets, stabilised by citric acid, followed by dip coating onto a copper foil and exposure to a strong magnetic field. The Fe₃O₄/GO sheets wrinkle along the vertical magnetic field lines, enhancing vertical pathways for charge transport. After annealing at 550 °C to reduce the GO sheets, W-Fe₃O₄/RGO anodes are obtained, further improving electron and ion kinetics.

The magnetic microrods align in an applied magnetic field with their long axis in the field direction, whereas the magnetic droplets form aligned porosity via particle chaining in the field direction. In both cases, after the magnetic phase has been oriented, the solvent phases are evaporated to leave a solid electrode [55]. In the microrod approach, the sacrificial phase is pyrolysed, whereas in the magnetic emulsion approach the emulsion phase is simply removed by drying. Under heightened magnetic fields, the magnetic force driving the particles to chain can be strong enough to also induce coalescence of the droplets into larger spherical droplets, so polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) is used as the stabiliser.

While these methods yield ultralow tortuosity and high areal capacity, their scalability depends on the availability of suitable magnetic templates, the strength and uniformity of the applied field over large areas, and additional processing steps [56]. Equipment costs for high-field magnets and throughput limitations currently restrict these techniques to proof-of-concept and niche applications; integration into roll-to-roll manufacturing would require template recovery or continuous-template strategies to minimise waste and cycle costs [57]. Overall, magnetically templated sacrificial phases represent a viable route for high-performance, low-tortuosity electrodes in advanced research and development settings, but significant engineering development is needed before they can compete commercially with simpler templating or template-free approaches.

5.2. 3D Printed Electrodes

3D printing has revolutionised electrode fabrication by allowing the layer-by-layer deposition of bespoke “inks”, from carbon precursors and conductive additives to wood-derived material, into finely controlled architectures that conventional casting cannot match. Techniques like direct ink writing (DIW) [58] produce electrodes with precisely tailored pore geometries and vertically aligned channels, cutting tortuosity from 5.3 to 2.1 [59] for faster ion transport, higher capacitance, and rapid charge/discharge. Researchers can formulate printable “inks” that incorporate carbon precursors, conductive additives, and even wood-derived materials [60]. These inks are extruded layer by layer through fine nozzles, allowing for precise control over the pore geometry, channel orientation, and overall electrode morphology [61]. By integrating wood-derived carbons, these methods yield binder-free, self-supporting structures that retain wood’s hierarchical porosity while boosting mechanical strength and conductivity. Moreover, 3D printing scales from ultrathin films (10 µm) to ultrathick blocks (1 mm), enabling graded, multilayer designs that balance energy and power densities. Challenges remain in formulating printable, stable inks and ensuring uniform microstructures over large areas, but advances in postprocessing, like controlled carbonisation and green activation, promise to further enhance both performance and sustainability [62]. Emerging trends focus on multi-material printheads for on-the-fly composition gradients and on aqueous or room-temperature inks that eliminate post-print thermal treatments, enhancing sustainability and integration into flexible substrates. With these advances, 3D printing is poised to revolutionise how we design and manufacture next-generation, low-tortuosity energy-storage electrodes.

While 3D printing has revolutionised electrode design with its versatility, integrating wood-derived materials into this process presents new opportunities and challenges that are currently under exploration. However, achieving the necessary ink rheology and maintaining consistent microstructure over larger formats remain key challenges for scaling these advances.

6. Realising the Potential of Wood-Derived Electrodes

6.1. Applications in Technologies

Optimising thick wood-derived carbon electrodes remains a challenge due to uneven loading of active materials and inefficient use of pore space. However, advancements in templating methods and controlled carbonisation techniques have improved the structural integrity and electrochemical performance of these battery electrodes; combined with the precision of direct ink binding, this can produce binder-free, self-supporting electrodes. Lightweight batteries for electric vehicles (EVs) and unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) are a particularly promising application of wood-templated electrodes, since every gram saved boosts both energy efficiency and operational range. For instance, conventional LiCoPO₄-based UAV batteries reach only 112.9 Wh kg⁻¹ [63], whereas wood-templated electrodes have demonstrated energy densities up to approximately 350 Wh kg⁻¹ [64]. The naturally hierarchical porosity of wood-derived carbon facilitates fast ion transport, resulting in high power output, making it a strong candidate for next-generation EV batteries. Additionally, the sustainable nature of wood-derived electrodes aligns with the growing demand for eco-friendly and recyclable energy storage solutions. By further refining electrode fabrication methods, wood-templated materials could play a crucial role in the development of high-performance, lightweight, and sustainable battery technologies.

They’re also useful in wearable electronics such as implantable batteries in portable medical equipment; their porous structure enhances heat dissipation, reducing the likelihood of thermal runaway, a common safety concern in traditional batteries. Their natural flexibility further enhances mechanical stability, ensuring reliable performance in wearables like continuous glucose monitors, ECG patches, and smart textiles, which require durability under constant movement.

6.2. Integration into Commercial Systems

The use of wood as a template for energy storage materials, while promising, presents several challenges. One primary issue is the structural variability of natural wood, which can lead to inconsistencies in the resulting carbon structure — an issue that is solved simply at laboratory scale. This variation, however, greatly affects electrochemical performance and reproducibility, making it difficult to scale up for commercial applications.

Another challenge is the high-temperature carbonisation process required to convert wood into functional electrode materials. This process consumes significant energy to reach and maintain 1,000 °C and can lead to structural collapse or loss of desired porosity if not carefully controlled. From a manufacturing standpoint, integrating wood-templated electrodes into existing battery and SC technologies remains a hurdle. Current commercial batteries rely on well-established electrode fabrication techniques, and transitioning to a wood-based template may require substantial process modifications.

Looking ahead, research should focus on improving the structural uniformity of wood-derived materials, optimising carbonisation techniques, and finding greener activation methods. Future developments could include hybrid approaches that combine wood with other templating materials to enhance performance while maintaining sustainability.

7. Research Gaps and Future Directions

Despite advancements in wood-derived energy storage materials, several research gaps persist that must be addressed to fully realise their potential.

One significant gap is the need for a deeper understanding of the structure-property relationships in wood-derived electrodes. While various wood species and processing conditions have been explored in Section 3.1, there is still limited knowledge on how these factors influence electrochemical properties such as conductivity, porosity, and mechanical stability. A systematic study, using mercury porosimetry and four-point probe measurements on at least three wood species, would establish clear performance-structure maps. By mapping the relationships between wood topography and electrochemical performance, researchers can pinpoint the key factors that most influence the outcome of a material.

Long-term stability also remains a concern, particularly for applications in batteries. While initial charge-discharge performance may be satisfactory, maintaining stability over thousands of cycles is vital for real-world applications. To address these challenges, optimising the infiltration and calcination parameters can help improve the overall sustainability of the process. For example, lowering the sintering temperature while maintaining structural integrity could reduce energy consumption, and employing greener solvents for sol–gel synthesis may lessen the environmental impact. By leveraging wood as a structural template, researchers can develop cost-effective and scalable electrode fabrication techniques while maintaining sustainability and high electrochemical efficiency. Investigating degradation mechanisms, with in-situ X-ray tomography during cycling to directly observe pore collapse and crack formation, and developing protective coatings or structural modifications could help address this issue.

Scaling up production is another pressing issue: to meet future goals, grid-scale storage needs to grow from 11 GW today to 970 GW [65], and at a typical four-hour discharge duration, the IEA’s Net Zero pathway implies on the order of 3.9 TWh of energy storage by 2030 to balance renewable variability [66]. However, as research efforts focus on the potential of wood-derived energy storage, they do not contextualise the issue in a large-scale manufacturing setting, making their future potential uncertain. Process optimisation and cost-effective synthesis routes must be developed to ensure that wood-derived electrodes can compete with existing materials in terms of affordability and commercial usage.

Finally, a full cradle-to-grave life-cycle assessment is needed to quantify the environmental impacts of wood-templated supercapacitors relative to conventional activated-carbon devices. Such an LCA should encompass sustainable forestry practices, chemical inputs during fabrication, and end-of-life recycling or disposal pathways [67]. By calculating a “carbon payback time” [68], the point at which the initial embodied carbon is offset by in-service benefits, researchers can determine whether and under what conditions wood-derived SCs provide a net environmental advantage.

8. Conclusion

Wood-templated electrodes offer an exciting pathway toward sustainable energy storage, leveraging the natural hierarchical structure of wood to create high-performance materials. Their use in SCs and batteries has demonstrated promising electrochemical performance, but key challenges remain in terms of reproducibility, scalability, and integration into commercial systems.

Wood-templated electrodes inherently trade off volumetric energy density for ultralow tortuosity and exceptional rate capability. By engineering the balance between pore volume and solid loading, through controlled infill, activation, or gradient structures, it is possible to approach or match the volumetric energy densities of conventional electrodes while retaining the kinetic advantages of the wood-derived channel network. Furthermore, the advancement of wood-derived thick electrodes has markedly enhanced energy density, due to the high concentration of electroactive sites within their porous architecture, and improved power density, enabled by the aligned and adjustable ion diffusion pathways [69].

Overall, while challenges exist, the potential of wood-derived materials in energy storage is significant, offering a renewable and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional electrode materials. Continued advancements in this field could contribute to the development of next-generation batteries and SCs with enhanced performance and sustainability.
